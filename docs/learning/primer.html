The one question

Does NIST's April 2026 triage leave exploited vulnerabilities without product data when defenders need it, and would a published re-check rule plus quarterly performance metrics close that gap at a cost NIST can absorb?

Today, NIST prioritizes byWho uses the software, or confirmed attacks
The project proposesRe-check the rest when attack signals rise, and publish whether it works

What changed in this revision. The evidence review narrowed the gap to product data (CPE), the one field nobody else supplies for free. It also changed the fix: forecast scores work poorly for screening brand-new flaws, but they work well for pulling stalled flaws back into the queue. The "8.4%" headline is now shown as disputed, and the PHP2Shell dates are corrected.

The warning system in one picture

A flaw passes three handoffs before defenders get a useful warning. Handoff 2 tells scanners which products are affected. Handoff 3, the CISA confirmed-attack list, now gates handoff 2 for most software, and it is getting slower.

Part 1

Who is involved

Written in ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English

NIST

Runs the NVD

A Commerce Department agency with about 21 NVD analysts. It adds product data and scores to CVEs. Since April 2026, it does this first for priority software.

CISA

Runs KEV, funds CVE

The U.S. cyber defense agency. It keeps the KEV list of attacked flaws. It also pays for the CVE Program, so it can set rules for the data in each CVE.

MITRE and the CVE BoardNEW

Sets CVE rules

MITRE operates the CVE Program under a CISA contract. The CVE Board advises it. Together they write the rules that CNAs must follow.

CNAs and AI labs

Feed the system

CNAs give each flaw its ID. Most are software vendors. The Linux kernel CNA is the largest. AI labs now report flaws in large batches.

FIRSTNEW

Runs EPSS

A global nonprofit of security teams. It publishes EPSS, the attack forecast score. Any EPSS-based rule depends on FIRST's model.

FedRAMP (GSA)NEW

Sets compliance rules

It sets security rules for cloud companies that sell to the government. Its 2026 rules already use KEV and EPSS to set deadlines.

State and local governmentsNEW

Feel the gap

About 19,000 of them used MS-ISAC, which lost federal funding in 2025. Like hospitals and small businesses, many cannot buy private data.

Congress

Funds and oversees

Appropriations, Science, and Homeland Security committees. In 2025, two ranking members asked GAO to review CVE and the NVD.

Part 2

What is happening, and what we build

The story in dates

  1. Feb 2024The NVD backlog starts after an enrichment contract lapses. This is before the AI surge.
  2. Dec 10, 2024CISA's Vulnrichment program stops adding product data (CPE). NIST becomes the only free federal source.
  3. Apr 15, 2026NIST prioritizes KEV, federal, and EO 14028 critical software. It no longer routinely re-scores CVEs that a CNA already scored. The goal for KEV flaws is one business day.
  4. May 26, 2026The Commerce Inspector General cites poor planning. NIST's re-scores matched independent scores in only 8 of 69 samples.
  5. Apr–Jun 2026Volerion counts about 50% of new CVEs enriched, but NIST scored only about 20% itself.
  6. Jul 4, 2026FedRAMP's 2026 rules (optional) set deadlines using KEV, EPSS, and reachability.
  7. Jul 24, 2026Barracuda: the median time from CVE to KEV listing grew from 6 days (2023) to 14 days (2026).
  8. Aug 12, 2026NIST asks the public: what metrics should measure NVD success? (RFI question 7(e))
  9. Oct 21–26, 2026PHP2Shell: probes from Oct 17, patch Oct 21, confirmed attacks Oct 24–25, KEV Oct 26.
  10. Oct 23, 2026One analysis estimates only 8.4% of Q3 CVEs were enriched. This conflicts with Volerion. The two use different definitions.

What the website does

1 · THE GAP

Product-data (CPE) coverage by priority tier, counted from the NVD API with the definition shown.

2 · THE GATE

How long CVEs wait for KEV, as a yearly trend, with PHP2Shell as the worked example.

3 · THE FIX AND ITS COST

A re-check rule simulator: CVEs added per month, share of NIST's capacity, and cost against the Inspector General's savings.

Part 3

Why: the argument, one claim at a time

The project rests on five claims. Each one stands on specific sources, including the ones that push back. Open a claim to see what each source says in plain language. RAND work is tagged; sources that challenge the claim are tagged in red.

1AI is deepening a capacity gap that began in 2024
NIST NVD operations updateNIST · Apr 15, 2026
CVE submissions rose 263% from 2020 to 2025. NIST enriched about 42,000 CVEs in 2025, a record, and still fell behind. The notice does not mention AI. Proves: the volume problem is real and older than AI.
Vulnerability trends in the AI eraGoogle Threat Intelligence Group · Sep 30, 2026
Monthly CVEs went from 5,045 (January) to 10,740 (August 2026). Only 0.23% were seen exploited. About 5,000 Linux kernel CVEs came from automatic numbering, not AI. Proves: volume is exploding, but most CVEs are never attacked.
AI agents put offensive cyber within reach of novicesRANDRR-A3892-2 · June 2026
An AI agent solved three hacking tests in under an hour each, for about $16 total. People with 2025 chatbots mostly failed. Proves: attack skill is no longer a barrier.
Maintainers on the front linecurl (Stenberg, Oct 22, 2026) · Linux (Torvalds, May 2026)
curl gets 4–5 times more reports than in 2024, and most are now real. Torvalds called the kernel security list "almost entirely unmanageable." Proves: AI reports are high volume and high quality, so triage matters.
2026 vulnerability forecast updateChallengesFIRST · Jun 15, 2026
Volume runs about 46% above forecast, partly from GitHub advisories and a numbering authority of last resort, not AI. Flaws that are actually attacked or likely to be attacked stay flat. Challenges: the "AI flood" framing. Say "accelerates," not "causes."
2The missing piece is product data, not severity scores
Volerion analysisvia Dark Reading · Apr 15–Jun 15, 2026
Of 13,441 new CVEs, NIST prioritized 62% and enriched about 50%. NIST gave its own score to only about 20%. Median time to analysis was about 4 days. Proves: NIST still processes many CVEs, but scores itself far fewer.
"The Count Broke First"DisputedCyber Strategy Institute · Oct 23, 2026
Estimates 8.4% enriched in Q3 2026. Its 87% figure for 2025 likely divides NIST's yearly output by yearly volume, which is not a coverage rate. Disputed: it conflicts with Volerion. Show both, with definitions.
CPE coverage and VulnrichmentVulnCheck via CSA · CISA GitHub
NVD had product data for 41% of 2024 CVEs, against 77% from a commercial service. CISA stopped adding product data in December 2024. Vendor records do not include it. Proves: product data has no free substitute.
Evaluation of NVD managementCommerce OIG · OIG-26-020-I · May 26, 2026
80% of CVEs already arrive with a vendor score. NIST's re-scores matched independent scores in only 8 of 69 samples. Re-scoring wasted about $200,000. Proves: dropping NIST re-scores is defensible. Don't attack that half of the policy.
The NVD shell gameBrian Martin, jericho.blog · May 7, 2026
NVD's status labels changed on April 15, and "Not Scheduled" counts fell by about 77,000 with no clear explanation. The widely cited "29,000 moved" figure comes from CSA, not NIST. Proves: the numbers can't be compared across April 2026 without care.
3The KEV gate is slow, slowing, and narrow
The KEV gapBarracuda · Jul 24, 2026
Median days from CVE publication to KEV listing: 6 (2023), 9 (2024), 12 (2025), 14 (2026). Proves: NIST's main trigger is getting slower every year.
State of Exploitation 2026VulnCheck · Jan 21, 2026
VulnCheck logged 884 newly exploited CVEs in 2025; CISA's KEV added 245. 29% were exploited on or before the day the CVE was published. Proves: KEV misses most exploited flaws. Note: VulnCheck sells the alternative.
Time-to-exploit trendsMandiant · Oct 2024; M-Trends · Mar 2026
Average time to exploit fell from 63 days (2018–19) to 5 days (2023), or about 47 with outliers included. M-Trends 2026 puts the mean at −7 days: attacks now often start before the patch. Mandiant does not blame AI. Proves: the warning window is closing.
PHP2Shell caseRecorded Future · BleepingComputer · CISA · Oct 2026
Probes Oct 17 (unsuccessful), patch Oct 21, confirmed attacks Oct 24–25, KEV Oct 26: nine days after the first probes, one to two days after confirmed attacks. Proves: how KEV timing works. PHP was likely already a priority as federally used software.
Zero Days, Thousands of NightsRANDRR-1751 · 2017
Flaws live an average of 6.9 years. Proves: old, stalled CVEs stay dangerous, which is why a re-check rule matters.
4Attack signals can re-check stalled CVEs, though they can't screen new ones
EPSSRAND authorJacobs, Romanosky et al. · 2019; v3 paper 2023
Co-created by Sasha Romanosky, then at RAND. At a 0.10 threshold, EPSS flagged 2.7% of CVEs and caught 63% of exploited ones. A "CVSS 7 or higher" rule flagged 57% to catch 82%. Proves: forecasts are far more efficient than severity alone.
The KEV gapChallengesBarracuda · Jul 24, 2026
EPSS crossed 0.10 before the KEV listing in only 19% of cases. But when it did, it led by an average of 201 days. Challenges: using EPSS to screen new CVEs. Supports: using it to re-check older, stalled ones.
EPSS critique and FAQChallengesParla, arXiv · 2024 · FIRST FAQ
Many KEV CVEs had low EPSS scores before listing, so Parla calls EPSS "a trailing indicator." FIRST says 0.10 "carries no special authority" and KEV should win when they disagree. Challenges: any single fixed threshold.
Likely Exploited VulnerabilitiesNIST CSWP 41 · Mell and Spring · May 2025
NIST's own paper proposes a combined score: the highest of EPSS, KEV, and a new "likely exploited" measure. It is not yet tested on real data. Proves: the re-check rule can use a measure NIST already designed.
FedRAMP 2026 rulesGSA FedRAMP · optional from Jul 4, 2026
Sets fix deadlines with KEV, EPSS, and reachability instead of NVD scores. Proves: the federal government already uses these signals.
5Nobody measures the warning system consistently
RFI: Modernizing the NVDNIST · 91 FR 52042 · Aug 12, 2026
Question 7(e) asks what metrics should track NVD success. Comments closed Oct 13. NIST has published no follow-up. Its only public target is one business day for KEV flaws, and nobody checks it. Proves: NIST itself has an open question the scorecard answers.
Coverage disagreementVolerion vs. Cyber Strategy Institute
Two analyses of the same database in the same year differ by about 6 times, because no official definition exists. Proves: the strongest case for published, stable metrics.
AI agents and novicesRANDRR-A3892-2 · June 2026
One-time cyber risk assessments go stale fast, so risk needs continuous measurement. Proves: a weekly scorecard is the right format.
To Disclose, or Not to DisclosePardee RANDPolley dissertation · 2022
The government's vulnerability process lacked public reporting and accountability. Proves: public metrics are a known gap.
NIST can't keep upToomey, Just Security · Apr 27, 2026
The one policy essay found that treats the NVD as public infrastructure. It argues funding should scale with CVE volume. Proves: the policy literature is thin, which leaves room for this project.
Part 4

What the fix costs

Rough estimates. NIST publishes no per-CVE cost, so these use about $90 per CVE (CISA's former $3.8M a year divided by 42,000 CVEs) and NIST's demonstrated ceiling of about 5,000 CVEs a month.

OptionCVEs addedShare of capacityCrude cost
Re-check rule, EPSS ≥ 0.10, at 2026 average volume~250 / month~5%~$270K / yr
Same, at October 2026 volume~470 / month~9%~$510K / yr
Quarterly performance metrics——near zero
Restore enrichment for every CVEall—"tens of millions" (unsourced)
Offset: Inspector General's estimate of funds NIST could redirect by cutting redundant re-scoring——~$400K / yr

The takeaway: a re-check rule costs about what the Inspector General says NIST already wastes. The ask is a rule change, not new money.

What the evidence does not prove yet

  • Coverage figures conflict. The live tool must compute its own from the NVD API and state its definition.
  • EPSS v4 uses vendor data when NVD data is missing, but nobody has measured its accuracy on unenriched CVEs.
  • Cost figures use a crude per-CVE proxy and a 2023 EPSS rate. Treat them as upper bounds.
  • No source reports a breach caused by a missing NVD label. The case rests on lost coverage and time, not on a known incident.
  • Some sources sell alternatives (VulnCheck, Volerion) or advocate (CSA). GPT-6 Cyber figures are press-reported only. "V-etalon," NIST's reported AI tool, appears in one source.
Part 5

Terms, in plain language

The words an audience needs. Each has a comparison you can say out loud.

Vulnerability / exploit / patch
A flaw in software; a method that uses the flaw to break in; the fix.

Like a broken lock, a lock-pick for it, and a new lock.

CVE Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures
A unique ID for one public flaw.

Like a case number at a police station.

CNA CVE Numbering Authority
An organization allowed to assign CVE IDs. Often the vendor of the flawed product.

Like a car maker issuing its own recall notices.

NVD National Vulnerability Database
NIST's free public database that adds details to each CVE.

Like a library catalog card for every book.

Enrichment
Adding the severity score, affected products, and flaw type to a CVE.

Like a nutrition label. Without it, you know the name but not what's inside.

CPE product data · the key field
A standard name for each affected product and version. Scanners use it to match flaws to your systems.

Like a car's VIN. A recall without VINs can't tell you if your car is affected.

CVSS severity score, 0–10
How bad a flaw would be if used. Vendors usually supply one now.

Like a hurricane category. It measures strength, not whether it hits your town.

KEV Known Exploited Vulnerabilities
CISA's list of flaws confirmed to be under attack.

Like a police list of confirmed break-in methods, published after the break-ins.

EPSS Exploit Prediction Scoring System
FIRST's daily score for the chance a flaw is attacked in the next 30 days.

Like a weather forecast's chance of rain.

LEV NIST CSWP 41
NIST's proposed "likely exploited" measure, built from a flaw's EPSS history.

Like a doctor reading your fever chart over a week, not one temperature.

Vulnrichment / SSVC
CISA's program that adds a decision rating (SSVC) to CVEs, such as whether attack code exists. It no longer adds product data.

Like a triage nurse's tag in an emergency room.

"Not Scheduled"
NVD's label for CVEs it has no plan to enrich, unless someone asks.

Like a repair ticket filed in the "someday" drawer.

FedRAMP
The program that sets security rules for cloud services sold to the government.

Like a health inspector for government cloud vendors.

RFI Request for information
A formal public request where an agency asks for ideas before it decides.

Like a town hall in writing.

Framework
Leading vs. lagging signal
A leading signal predicts a problem. A lagging signal confirms it afterward.

Like a smoke detector versus a fire report.

Framework
Public good
Something everyone uses but no single buyer wants to fund alone.

Like a lighthouse. Every ship uses it; no ship pays for it.

Framework
Gatekeeping
When one signal decides who gets service. Here, KEV decides which flaws get product data.

Like an ER that treats only patients an ambulance brought in.

Framework
Continuous measurement
Tracking performance on a schedule instead of in one-time studies.

Like a fitness tracker versus a yearly physical.

Questions to expect

Didn't NIST still enrich about half of new CVEs after April?

Yes, according to Volerion for April to June. Another analysis says 8.4% for Q3. They define enrichment differently, and nobody publishes an official number. That disagreement is the argument for the scorecard. The live tool counts product data by tier, with its definition shown.

EPSS often rises only after attacks start. Why use it?

Correct for new CVEs, so the project no longer uses it to screen them. For older, stalled CVEs, EPSS led KEV by about 201 days when it led at all. NIST's own CSWP 41 already combines EPSS, KEV, and a "likely exploited" score. The rule uses that measure for the backlog.

Isn't this just a request for more money?

No. The rule costs roughly $270K to $510K a year on a crude estimate. The Inspector General found about $400K a year NIST could redirect from redundant re-scoring. It is a rule change paid for by existing waste.

Why not let the private market provide this data?

Commercial services already cover more product data than NVD. Large organizations buy them. State and local governments lost MS-ISAC funding in 2025, and hospitals and small businesses often can't pay. A free baseline protects them. RAND's RR-1700 notes that markets underinvest in security.

Is AI the cause of all this?

No. The backlog began in February 2024 when a contract lapsed. NIST cites a 263% rise in submissions without mentioning AI. AI makes an existing capacity problem worse and makes triage permanent.

Is this only about government software?

No. Government agencies run the system, so they can change it. Almost every scanner and compliance program reads NVD data, so the effects reach everyone. The software outside the federal tiers is exactly where the gap is.

Part 7

The whole project, for a beginner

Think of the NVD as a recall notice service for software. When someone finds a flaw, NIST adds a list of the affected products. Security scanners read that list to tell you whether your computers are affected.

Flaws now arrive faster than NIST can list them. So in April 2026, NIST decided to list products mainly for flaws in government software, or for flaws CISA has confirmed are under attack. Everything else may wait a long time, or never get a list.

That makes CISA's confirmed-attack list the gate for most software. The gate is slow, and it is getting slower: the typical wait grew from 6 days in 2023 to 14 days in 2026. It also misses many attacked flaws.

A forecast tool called EPSS, which a RAND researcher helped create, can't reliably spot danger on a flaw's first day. But it is good at noticing when old, stalled flaws start to heat up. NVD Watch proposes a simple rule: when attack signals rise, put the stalled flaw back in line. It also proposes five public numbers to show whether the system works, which answers a question NIST asked the public in August.
